Mudskipper.

Meet the namesake

A fish between worlds.

Mudskippers are a group of amphibious gobies: fish adapted to a life that crosses the water’s edge. The name covers multiple species with different habits, rather than one universal kind of fish. Their mudflat lives have made them a fascinating subject for researchers studying how vertebrates cope with land. Read the research overview.

Across tidal flats and around mangroves, a mudskipper can look surprisingly at home out of water. Strong pectoral fins help support and propel the body across a surface in a movement often called “crutching.” Research comparing movement in water and on land found that the fins change their motion to suit each setting. See Pace and Gibb’s locomotion study.

Air breathing still depends on moisture. Wet skin provides a surface for gas exchange, and keeping it damp matters. In experiments with Periophthalmus modestus, drier air prompted more rolling and fin movements that wet the skin. Explore the skin-wetting study.

Real mudskippers also dig burrows for shelter. Meet them at The Florida Aquarium. In Mudskipper: Build Me a River, river building is our playful metaphor for finding a way through a changing landscape. The fish supplies the inspiration; shaping an entire river belongs to the game.
